## Critical Introduction to "On Imagination"


   &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Phillis Wheatley’s rise to fame is a direct result of her ability to subtly critique American society, without being too brash or blunt. She cleverly incorporates her emotions and viewpoints in her poetry, illustrating the significance of her mental faculties in comparison to normal standards of artistic intellect, typically involving white male individuals rather than a black woman (the first in the literary field to publish a book) that courageously creates an identity for herself that does not center solely on slavery. In her poem “On Imagination” Phillis Wheatley uses symbolism to depict imagination as a mighty force of liberation, thereby providing her power and agency through her poetry by using nature as a tool of freedom to express criticism against the practices of black slavery during a time of great oppression and despicable cruelty towards black society.

   &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the poem “On Imagination” Phillis Wheatley effectively incorporates symbolism to depict how imagination does not require permission to be free from the shackles of a reality that considers her an anomaly instead of a genius poet who deserves recognition, respect and complete autonomy for her work rather than be at the mercy of a dominant white society who ardently desires to see her suppress her voice on every page. In her mind Phillis can do as she pleases without the restraints of slavery to bind her to the unjust rules of white supremacy. This is evident when Phillis depicts imagination as an “imperial queen” (line 1). By equating imagination to the figure of an “imperial queen” Phillis highlights the power of the human imagination, which answers to no one. Furthermore, a queen possesses ultimate superiority over others. Likewise, Phillis’s talents as a writer prove that she is not inferior to anyone, especially a dominating white patriarchal society that seeks to simply dismiss her expertise as a mere stroke of luck rather than a skill worth taking further so she can publish more books of poetry and garner further fame instead of retreating deeply into the shadows as she did near the end of her existence.

   &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In contrast, while imagination is liberating, it can also be quite constraining, holding the mind and body captive to the desires of the imagination. Phillis demonstrates this when she uses diction such as “silken fetters” (line 11).  Fetters are chains around the feet or ankles, restricting movement. By describing the fetters as “silken” instead of using iron Phillis depicts gentle restraint alongside captivity, illustrating the nature of Phillis’s writing which was soft in tone, but provided implicit messages of being bound in the shackles of slavery, while tasting a bittersweet sample of liberation simultaneously. Amoah-Ramey and Nana Abena state that “The conditions of her literacy were thus both enabling and constraining.” Even though Phillis was encouraged on an intellectual level by the Wheatley’s she was at the mercy of their rules, regulations, opinions, and decisions. She had virtually no autonomy over her career as a poet. However, the poem ends with a depressing message that centers on deliberately controlling the voice of imagination rather than allowing it to run wildly free. Phillis depicts this in the final line of the poem when the speaker states “Cease then, my song, cease the unequal lay” (line 53), by distinctly commanding the song to stop. The speaker cannot continue further until the desire for freedom comes true in the reality of their world. Alas, Phillis Wheatley uses the poem to express her desires for a united America that centers on equality rather than division.

## Contextual Accompaniment to "On Imagination"
<br>
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Previously, neoclassical poetry was usually written by white males possessing the privilege of wealth. Phillis Wheatley’s role as a black woman who was enslaved at the time of writing her book demonstrates that she continues to serve as an inspiration for other black writers who attempt to follow in her footsteps, without trespassing the boundaries of decency and contempt for the brutal history of the past involving the practices of black enslavement. Wheatley’s use of Greek and Roman mythology in her poem “On Imagination” demonstrates Wheatley’s brilliance as a neoclassical writer who strategically elevated herself amongst Western society by proving that she too is capable of a high intellect that centers on logic, restraint, and a striking balance by sticking to the traditional form of poetry rather than deviating from its original layout.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wheatley’s use of heroic couplets and mythological references depict the limitless power of the mind in comparison to the physical limits of a reality that seeks to actively constrain. For instance, the mention of Mount “*Helicon*” (line 5) and the fact that this was the home of the Muses in Greek mythology contains an obvious Western influence, in which Wheatley turns towards the "*Muses*" (line 49) for inspiration, as all the great poets before her have done so. The mountain represents an elevation of the mind and spirit for Wheatley, while the physical realm lies far below containing all types of boundaries at every step of the way. Moreover, according to Katarina O'Briain, “In that way, her poetry might offer an ideal example of what Wasserman would call “serious poetry”: a mode of heroic-couplet thinking that seeks, as always, “to call into valid being a particular ordering of reality.”” Phillis uses the form of heroic couplets to intentionally create tension between imposing order in America versus infinite freedom in the condition of the human imagination. She not only proves mastery by consistently incorporating this form; she challenges the assumption that black individuals, especially women lack any talent for poetry that involves strategic rhyme and diction. Consequently, Phillis Wheatley redefines the typical identity of a poet by the struggles that shape her during the process of eventually securing publication for her book and creating a legacy for future black writers who strive to aim high but never fail to acknowledge the immense power of liberty.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Furthermore, Wheatley’s personification of nature induces a subtle effect that seeks to alternate between the notions of captivity and liberation. Wheatley demonstrates this when she personifies the season of winter as the speaker states, “*Winter* austere forbids me to aspire” (line 50). The cold symbolizes death and decay, resulting in a barren landscape, devoid of fertile growth. On the contrary, the Roman goddess “*Flora*” (line 27) represents a new beginning, hope, and happiness in the form of Spring, abundant with various vibrant flowers that contain a seed of opportunity for a better future that aims for inclusion rather than constant repulsion. In addition, O”Briain states that “In the case of a poet like Phillis Wheatley, limits have long been placed around her work — both because of when she was writing and because of who she was” (2). Phillis’s identity as the first black woman to ever publish a book of poems suggests that she must have had several restrictions placed upon her to stop her from condemning the society that aided in her discovery, by encouraging her education instead of completely concealing the knowledge of literacy from her. However, during her final days such support vanished only to be replaced with a feeling of being utterly lost among the crowd amidst the many faces that are only silent and do not speak the language of a neoclassical tone using heroic couplets as fluently as she does. Hence, by empowering others to look beyond themselves into the future of ultimate liberation and equality, she implicitly demands justice in the face of continuous oppression.

## ON IMAGINATION.
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;THY various works, imperial queen, we see,
<br>How bright their forms! how deck’d with pomp by thee!
<br>Thy wond’rous acts in beauteous order stand,
<br>And all attest how potent is thine hand.

> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From *Helicon’s* [^1] refulgent[^2] heights attend,
<br>Ye sacred choir, and my attempts befriend:
<br>To tell her glories with a faithful tongue,
<br>Ye blooming graces, triumph in my song.
 
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Now here, now there, the roving[^3] *Fancy* [^4] flies,
<br>Till some lov’d object strikes her wand’ring eyes,
<br>Whose silken fetters[^5] all the senses bind,
<br>And soft captivity involves the mind.
    
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*Imagination*! who can sing thy force?
<br>Or who describe the swiftness of thy course?
<br>Soaring through air to find the bright abode,
<br>Th’ empyreal palace of the thund’ring God,
<br>We on thy pinions[^6] can surpass the wind,
<br>And leave the rolling universe behind:
<br>From star to star the mental optics rove,
<br>Measure the skies, and range the realms above.
<br>There in one view we grasp the mighty whole,
<br>Or with new worlds amaze th’ unbounded soul.
  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Though *Winter* frowns to *Fancy’s* raptur’d eyes
<br>The fields may flourish, and gay scenes arise;
<br>The frozen deeps may break their iron bands,
<br>And bid their waters murmur o’er the sands.
<br>Fair *Flora* [^7] may resume her fragrant reign,
<br>And with her flow’ry riches deck the plain;
<br>*Sylvanus*[^8] may diffuse his honours round,
<br>And all the forest may with leaves be crown’d:
<br>Show’rs may descend, and dews their gems disclose,
<br>And nectar sparkle on the blooming rose.
  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Such is thy pow’r, nor are thine orders vain,
>br>O thou the leader of the mental train:
<br>In full perfection all thy works are wrought,
<br>And thine the sceptre o’er the realms of thought.
<br>Before thy throne the subject-passions bow,
<br>Of subject-passions sov’reign ruler thou;
<br>At thy command joy rushes on the heart,
<br>And through the glowing veins the spirits dart.

> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*Fancy* might now her silken pinions try
<br>To rise from earth, and sweep th’ expanse on high:
<br>From *Tithon’s* bed[^9] now might *Aurora*[^10] rise,
<br>Her cheeks all glowing with celestial dies,
<br>While a pure stream of light o’erflows the skies.
<br>The monarch of the day I might behold,
<br>And all the mountains tipt with radiant gold,
<br>But I reluctant leave the pleasing views,
<br>Which *Fancy* dresses to delight the *Muse*; [^11]
<br>*Winter* austere forbids me to aspire,
<br>And northern tempests damp the rising fire;
<Br>They chill the tides of *Fancy’s* flowing sea,
<br>Cease then, my song, cease the unequal lay[^12].

[^2]: means to shine radiantly
[^3]: the act of travelling without an aim
[^4]: another word for imagination
[^5]: chains or shackles that were placed around the ankles or feet, limiting movement

[^7]: the goddess of all plants, flowers, and spring in Roman mythology
[^8]: God of the wilderness, woods, and the forest in Roman mythology
[^9]: the marriage bed of and Tithon (the lover of Eos) and Eos (the goddess of dawn). Eos gave brightness to the world in the morning after rising from this bed.
[^10]: Known as the goddess of dawn in Roman mythology (the equivalent of the goddess Eos) It is also a vibrant display of natural colourful lights in the sky.
[^11]: They are the nine goddesses in Greek mythology pertaining to literature, sciences and the arts. In modern times it refers to a person or force who serves as a source of artistic inspiration for the artist. According to Greek mythology Mount Helicon (located in Boeotia, Greece) is the home of the Muses.
[^12]: a song or poem in 18th century literature.
